## Decision

1. **Option (a) of DEC-724 item 9, as the founder decided it.** The relay request carries the
   workspace deployment's VAPID `Authorization` header (RFC 8292 §3). The application server's
   private key stays with the deployment (behind `mandate_webpush::VapidSigner`, in its vault).
   This loosens CP-1 by exactly that one opaque field, `authorization` in `RelaySend`, and by
   nothing else. Option (b), a platform key at the relay, is not taken.
2. **The field is opaque to the relay.** The relay checks its shape (item 4) and nothing more: it
   does not decode the token, read a claim, check the signature or the expiry, or compare `k`
   with any key it knows. It holds no VAPID key and adds no `Authorization` of its own.
3. **Forwarded byte for byte, and never kept.** The relay sends the field, unchanged, as the
   `Authorization` header of its one `POST` (DEC-724 item 6), so the push service receives five
   things where DEC-724 item 5 named four: the endpoint, the pair's `urgency` and TTL, the
   ciphertext, and this header. The relay never logs, stores, echoes, or puts the field in a
   metric or an error. Each request still leaves exactly one `LogEntry`, the relay id and the
   closed answer (DEC-724 item 7), and no log or error type can hold the header. Its refusals are
   closed codes that carry no input. Nothing of it outlives the call. On the deployment side the
   header is built for each request by `mandate_webpush::build_request` with that attempt's time
   (so its `exp`, 12 hours on, never lapses inside a 24-hour `safety` retry window), is never
   reused across attempts, and is never journaled, logged, or put in a metric, as the address is
   not (NT-2).
4. **Its shape is checked, and capped at 1 024 octets.** The field must be exactly
   `vapid t=<a>.<b>.<c>, k=<d>`: the lowercase scheme `vapid`, one space, `t=`, three dot-separated
   segments, a comma and one space, `k=`, then one segment, and nothing before or after. Every
   segment is unpadded base64url (`A`–`Z`, `a`–`z`, `0`–`9`, `-`, `_`; no `=`); `<a>` and `<b>`
   are non-empty, `<c>` is exactly 86 characters (an ES256 signature, 64 octets), and `<d>` is
   exactly 87 characters (an uncompressed P-256 point, 65 octets). This is the form
   `mandate_webpush::vapid_authorization` already writes (DEC-790 item 4). The whole field is at
   most 1 024 octets. Anything else, including a field that is empty, is refused as
   `invalid_authorization`. **The cap's arithmetic:** the parts `vapid_authorization` fixes (the
   scheme and separators, its 36-character JWT header, the signature, and the key) are 223 octets,
   so 1 024 leaves 801 base64url characters, which hold 600 octets, for the claims
   `{"aud":"…","exp":…,"sub":"…"}`. With the longest origin an allowlisted endpoint can have
   (`https://`, a 253-octet host, `:65535`: 267 octets) and a 20-digit `exp`, a subject of up to
   287 octets fits. The founder's subject, `mailto:push@notify.owlhead.ai` (DEC-790 item 4), with
   `https://fcm.googleapis.com` gives a 345-octet header.
5. **Required on every relayed request; absent is refused.** Every v1 web-push subscription is made
   with the deployment's own application server key (spec §4.6; DEC-438 item 22, decided by the
   founder in DEC-824), so every relayed request needs the header, and the relay cannot tell a
   subscription made without one. A request with no `authorization`, or an empty one, is refused
   as `invalid_authorization` and nothing is posted. This replaces DEC-724 item 9's interim
   behaviour (forward with no `Authorization`). The reading adds no risk and is the tighter one:
   both readings lose that one push, which is only a missed interruption (NT-5) and touches no
   trade path (NT-9), but refusing sends nothing to a third party, keeps the request's shape closed
   with no optional field, and keeps the push service's `401` or `403` (RFC 8292 §4.2), which the
   deployment could read as a verdict on the address, from ever being caused by a deployment bug.
   Native push after v1 uses the relay's own app credentials (spec §4.6) and is not covered here.
6. **Order of checks.** DEC-724 item 8's order gains one step at its end: relay id, endpoint,
   envelope, size, then authorization. The first refusal wins, every check still runs before any
   post, and every existing refusal keeps its code for the same input.
7. **No other field.** `RelaySend` is `{relay_id, endpoint, urgency, ttl_s, authorization,
   ciphertext}`. No other field enters it, and none is optional. The ciphertext's 512-octet cap
   and the endpoint's 2 048-octet cap are unchanged, and the header's cap is its own; none of the
   three lends room to another.
8. **The residuals are stated, not hidden.** The header's claims are base64url, not encrypted, so
   the relay could read `aud` (the push service's origin, which the endpoint already shows), `exp`
   (the signing time plus a fixed 12 hours, never a deadline, so NT-1 holds), `sub` (the
   deployment's one contact URI), and `k` (the deployment's public key, which identifies a
   deployment the relay already knows from its mutual-TLS certificate). Item 2 has it read none of
   them. Within the shape and the cap, a compromised deployment could carry about 600 octets in
   the claims segment besides the 512-octet ciphertext and DEC-724 item 4's endpoint residual; the
   threat model owns that, as spec §9 says for a compromised deployment. A malicious relay operator
   holding a header until its `exp` could post to endpoints it has seen on that push service, but
   without the browser's keys it can send at most a push with no body or one the browser cannot
   decrypt, never our text or a link.

## Rationale

Spec §4.6 makes the deployment the VAPID signer, and the relay request had no field to carry the
signature, so a relayed push could only be refused by the push service. The founder chose to carry
the deployment's header rather than move the key. Items 2 to 7 keep the loosening to what item 1
names: one field, opaque, checked only for shape and size, forwarded unchanged, never kept, and
placed after every existing check so DEC-724's tests still describe the same refusals. Item 5
takes the reading that sends less, since both readings lose the push and neither touches trading
state. Item 8 writes down what the field reveals, so the canary tests can be aimed at it.

## Alternatives considered

- **(b) The relay signs with a platform key** (DEC-724 item 9). Not taken by the founder. Its cost:
  the browser would subscribe with the platform's public key rather than the deployment's, which
  changes §4.6's signer and the web client's subscription; every subscription made before the
  change would need renewing; one platform key would sign for every hybrid deployment, so its
  compromise would reach all of them; and the relay would hold a signing key, which CP-1's "keeps
  nothing" and §1.2's "credentials of any kind" would also have to loosen.
- **Forward a request with no header unchanged** (DEC-724 item 9's interim). Rejected by item 5: it
  sends a request the push service must refuse, and lets that refusal look like the address's
  fault.
- **The relay verifies the token** (signature against `k`, `aud` against the endpoint's origin,
  `exp` against its clock). Rejected: the founder's choice is an opaque field, and verifying it
  needs the relay to decode and interpret what the deployment sent. The push service verifies it
  anyway.
- **Accept any case, spacing, or parameter order RFC 8292 and HTTP allow.** Rejected: the one
  writer is `mandate_webpush::vapid_authorization`, so one exact form is enough and refuses more.
- **No cap, or the ciphertext's 512.** Rejected: an uncapped field would be a free-text channel
  through the control plane, and 512 would refuse a valid header for a long allowlisted origin.
